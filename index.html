Ph.D. Thesis

Automated Change Analysis in Regulatory Requirements Engineering

Parisa Elahidoost Mayr · Department of Software Engineering, Blekinge Tekniska Högskola (BTH)

This is a cumulative thesis. The research presented in this dissertation was conducted in close collaboration with an industrial partner in the financial sector.

Abstract

Background

Software-intensive systems in regulated domains must continuously adapt to evolving regulations. Regulatory changes may affect requirements, workflows, architecture, implementation, documentation, and testing. Analyzing these changes and their consequences is therefore an important part of maintaining regulatory compliance, yet it remains largely manual, knowledge-intensive, dependent on domain expertise, and prone to errors.

Problem

Existing research provides limited support for treating regulatory change analysis as an integrated software engineering activity, due to four main shortcomings:

  1. limited empirical understanding of how regulatory requirements and changes are handled in industrial practice;
  2. a lack of structured conceptualizations that distinguish different types of regulatory changes and support systematic reasoning about their engineering relevance;
  3. limited tool support for detecting, classifying, and interpreting regulatory changes in realistic regulatory contexts; and
  4. limited support for translating identified regulatory changes into impact assessments across software artifacts and engineering activities.

Together, these shortcomings create a gap between identifying changes in regulatory documents and systematically determining their consequences for software systems. At the same time, recent advances in Generative AI, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), provide new opportunities to bridge this gap through their ability to process and reason over large volumes of textual information. However, how effectively such capabilities can support regulatory change and impact analysis in software engineering remains insufficiently understood.

Goal

This cumulative thesis investigates the challenges software engineers face when analyzing evolving regulations and their impact on software systems, and the extent to which these challenges can be addressed through automated and, in particular, LLM-assisted approaches.

Method

The research follows an iterative Design Science Research approach. First, we synthesize existing research and investigate industrial practices to identify challenges in Regulatory Requirements Engineering. Second, we establish foundations for automation by deriving requirements for compliance automation systems and developing a taxonomy and conceptual model of regulatory changes. Third, we design and experimentally evaluate LLM-assisted methods for regulatory change analysis. Finally, we extend this support towards regulatory impact analysis by developing and evaluating a category-driven impact model and ReguLens, an evidence-grounded tool for translating detected regulatory changes into potentially affected software artifacts.

Results

Our research provides a consolidated understanding of Regulatory Requirements Engineering and empirical insights into industrial practices, challenges, and opportunities for automation. It establishes a structured set of engineering requirements for designing and evaluating human-centered compliance automation systems, alongside a taxonomy and conceptual model for systematically representing regulatory changes. The experimental evaluation shows that LLMs can support the detection and classification of recurring and structurally explicit regulatory changes, while performance decreases for narrative, weakly structured, or context-dependent changes, reinforcing the need for human involvement. The research further extends regulatory change analysis towards software impact assessment through a category-driven impact model and the ReguLens. The industrial evaluation provides first evidence that this approach can well support practitioners by providing structured starting points for implementation investigation, while also revealing limitations related to change granularity, the distinction between contextual conditions and implementation actions, and fine-grained localization of affected software artifacts.

Conclusion

The thesis establishes a systematic foundation for regulatory change and impact analysis and demonstrates how LLM-assisted and tool-supported approaches can augment human expertise within a structured engineering process. The findings position such approaches as decision support rather than autonomous compliance solutions, with practitioners retaining responsibility for interpreting regulatory changes, validating their impacts, and making implementation decisions.
